# Musical Computation (Separated Notes)
## Music Theory as Discrete Lattice and Possible Computational Use

**Status:** Separated from the quantums-of-signal framework to avoid confusion. Version: 2026-11-07.

Music theory supplies one historically privileged set of discrete levels (the 12-tone equal-tempered lattice, the circle of fifths, major/minor modes, tonic, intervals, time signatures). In a quantizer these levels are simply the allowed values of \(Q\).

### What maps reasonably
- Any chosen discrete subset of a continuum can serve as a state space.
- Harmonic vs. disharmonic, major vs. minor, can be treated as labels on attractors or as preferred vs. non-preferred fixed points.
- Continuous (soft or residual) quantization can snap a circulating signal onto a musical lattice exactly as it can snap onto any other lattice.

### What does not automatically follow
- Musical intervals do not by themselves constitute a general model of computation.
- Tonic, major third, minor third, etc. are not privileged computational results outside the musical domain.
- Time signatures and small integers used in music are discrete choices; they do not encode genetic codons or bit depth.

### Open questions (for later)
- Can a residual-driven loop whose allowed levels are a musical scale perform useful non-musical computation more easily than a generic lattice?
- Are there modular patches in which harmonic relationships are used as an explicit error signal or consistency condition?
- Historical evidence: have any synthesists or theorists published a worked example of “music theory as computation” that goes beyond metaphor?

These notes are kept separate so the signal-quantization framework remains unentangled from musical interpretation. Continuous quantization can still use a musical lattice when that lattice is the desired discrete set.
